The first quarter of 2026 began with monetary policy and tariffs in the spotlight and ends dominated by geopolitics: the closure of the Strait of Hormuz in early March has put energy back at the centre of the markets.

## Markets

**United States.** The Federal Reserve held rates at 3.50–3.75% at its 18 March meeting, in a more hawkish tone than expected: inflation is still proving sticky, and the uncertainty in the Middle East adds to the universal 10% tariff in force since 24 February. The nomination of Kevin Warsh as the next Fed chair, announced at the end of January, has added pressure on Treasuries.

**Europe.** The ECB kept the deposit rate at 2%. Inflation, around 1.9% in February, has jumped to 2.5% in March on higher energy prices, and the bank has raised its 2026 forecast to 2.6%. Expectations of rate cuts have cooled.

**China.** At the "Two Sessions" Beijing set a growth target of 4.5–5%, the lowest since 1991, with a message of continuity and more support for domestic consumption, but no large stimulus package.

**Hormuz.** After the attacks of 28 February, Iran declared the strait closed on 2 March and tanker traffic has fallen by more than 90%. Close to a fifth of the world's oil passes through Hormuz: Brent has approached $110 and global equities close the quarter lower after an optimistic start to the year.

## How the allocation has behaved

The quarter has rewarded exactly what the global index barely holds. Commodity and energy companies have risen strongly while global equities fell, and that is where much of the two value funds, **Cobas Selección** and **Azvalor Internacional**, is invested, with heavy exposure to energy and oil services. They have been by far the block that contributed most in the profiles with more equity.

The rest of the equity sleeve has suffered with the market: the **MSCI World** and **technology** felt the March fall, and **emerging markets** held up somewhat better.

In fixed income, the bet on **short duration** is doing its job: with the market no longer pricing rate cuts, the liquidity and short-term fixed income funds have barely moved, while longer-dated debt has felt it. Flexible credit has cushioned, though without shining.

## What it leaves us

That the diversification that matters is not the number of funds but the number of return drivers: in a quarter of energy shock, holding part of the portfolio in businesses that benefit from that shock changes the result against a global index completely.
